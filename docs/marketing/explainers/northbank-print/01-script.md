# Keeping the promise at Northbank Print

Northbank Print has twenty-two people, two sites and a delivery problem. Maya, its managing director, asks Stephen to make production more responsive. Rosa, who runs production, hears a request to work faster. Ben, who handles customers, hears a request to stop changing the dates. Imani, an operator and staff representative, wants to know who will absorb the extra work.

Stephen asks what customers should notice, and what employees must be able to rely on. They choose a shared goal: dispatch standard repeat orders when promised, within paid working time, while people can challenge unrealistic commitments without penalty. Bespoke work is outside this twelve-week engagement. The representatives check the wording with their teams.

The baseline is uncomfortable. Twenty of eighty orders went out late. Only twelve of forty changed-order requests received an authorized answer, communicated to the customer, by the next working day. Saying that a request has been received does not count as deciding it.

They agree what would be good enough: no more than ten percent late, prompt decisions on at least eighty percent of requests, and two consecutive qualifying fortnights. Overtime must stay within eighteen hours each week across the business. There must be no unpaid work. At least sixteen employees must say they can speak up, with at least eighteen of twenty eligible people responding.

Stephen combines interviews, records and an optional questionnaire. The office faces customer urgency; the workshop faces materials and capacity limits. The assessment considers coordination, adaptability, robustness and the changing environment. It has not selected a solution yet.

The first explanation says sales shares changes too slowly, leaving production with incomplete instructions. Ben challenges that connection. He brings emails showing that changes were sent promptly. The group agrees to trace three orders before assigning a sales fix.

On two of those orders, the information arrived promptly. The wait came later: only Maya could approve the changed delivery commitment. Nobody covered her absence. Some jobs entered production while the decision was still unresolved.

Stephen changes the explanation. Waiting for approval and releasing the job before resolution can together leave instructions incomplete. Incomplete instructions can contribute to late dispatches. Neither condition alone explains every late order. Idle press slots also weaken a blanket explanation about insufficient machinery.

The group chooses decision-making as its first coordination issue. A feedback loop will help them adapt; cover for Maya matters to robustness. They will trace ten more orders before trying an adjustment.

Stephen uses an ACT-informed discussion to explore what matters and what gets in the way. Ben values dependability, but fears losing customers when he asks for time. Rosa wants good work, but sometimes expects her objections to be dismissed. Imani reminds them that some requested dates really are impossible. Private personal disclosures stay private.

They choose a short morning review of changed orders. Maya expects to attend and authorize answers. Stephen draws the expectation: earlier discussion should enable decisions, complete instructions and fewer late dispatches. The group also keeps a harmful possibility visible: preparing for the meeting could extend beyond paid hours.

Six of ten additional changed jobs have unresolved decisions. The team prepares a shared list and paid meeting time. Maya will attend, but deputy cover is still missing. The weakness stays on the record.

Before starting on 1 February, they record a prediction: at least thirty-two of forty anticipated requests will receive prompt decisions, and no more than twelve of eighty orders will go out late. That trial target is less demanding than the original completion criterion. It does not replace it.

After one week, six of forty orders are late and seventeen of twenty decisions are reported prompt. Maya suggests expanding. Stephen asks for another week and a check of the definition. They continue the same trial.

Checking the timestamps changes the first week's decision count from seventeen to eleven. Some acknowledgments had counted as authorized answers. The original report is retained beside its correction.

The complete fortnight produces twenty-one prompt decisions out of forty, below thirty-two expected. Sixteen of eighty orders are late, above twelve expected. Eight of ten meetings took place. Maya missed two; paper supply also worsened.

Imani reports two preparation hours outside her paid shift. Once paid and counted, the second week's overtime rises from seventeen to nineteen hours, above the ceiling. Only ten people give a positive speaking-up answer; sixteen of twenty respond. Missing answers remain unknown. Expansion pauses.

An AI preparation brief calls staff reluctant to use the process. Imani objects: people used it, but nobody there could authorize an answer. Stephen withdraws the interpretation. A meeting cannot supply authority by itself.

Maya offers bounded delegation. Ben and Rosa can settle routine replans within written limits; the meeting will handle exceptions. A named deputy and paid preparation time must be available. The revised arrangement needs fresh agreement and its own trial.

Before 22 February, Maya defines the limits and names the deputy. Maya and Rosa allocate paid preparation time, taking two hours from nonurgent quotation work. Imani checks the burden. Quote delays remain something to watch.

The next report covers days before and after the revision. Fourteen of eighty orders are late and twenty-four of forty decisions are prompt. Those figures stay, but this mixed period cannot fairly evaluate the revised arrangement.

Before March begins, they record another expectation: at least thirty-two of forty prompt decisions, no more than eight of eighty late orders, and all the original safeguards. The observation window is 1–12 March.

That window brings thirty-four prompt decisions out of forty. While Maya is away for two days, five of six requests still receive prompt answers. Seventy-five of eighty jobs have complete instructions at their slot, but no comparable readiness baseline was collected.

Eight of eighty orders are late. Overtime is sixteen and fifteen hours. Sixteen people answer positively about speaking up; nineteen respond. No unpaid work is reported. The window qualifies, but paper supply is better and the jobs slightly easier. The arrows remain hypotheses. They continue unchanged.

The second March window brings thirty-five prompt decisions and seven late orders. Overtime is fourteen and fifteen hours; seventeen people answer positively, with nineteen responses. No unpaid work is reported. Both nonoverlapping windows meet the original criteria. Open questions about machinery, peak demand and quotation delays remain open.

Active support ends on 2 April. Rosa takes over the same record. Ben supplies order figures, Maya checks paid hours and Imani maintains the anonymous pulse and private route for concerns. Reviewed releases preserve what was agreed without replacing the working history.

Unpaid work or reprisal concerns require immediate review. Two consecutive failed delivery or voice windows invite Stephen back; the group can ask sooner.

In May, nine of eighty orders go out late. Prompt decisions still pass at thirty-three of forty. Overtime stays within limits, but only fifteen people answer positively about speaking up; eighteen respond. Deputy cover was reassigned twice, and a new customer sends more late changes. An employee says challenging dates feels like holding everyone up.

The group restores cover, offers a voluntary conversation and schedules 28 May to review again. This is one failed window, so the automatic two-window invitation has not fired. They have not abandoned March's results or explained away May's warning.

The next review has not happened.
