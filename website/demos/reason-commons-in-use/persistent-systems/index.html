From the documents to a shared question

Stephen Le’s Adaptive Persistence asks three questions of a group: can we cooperate, can we adapt, can we tolerate disruption? It turns the answers into five steps a group can take to match its capacities to its environment. This map follows that protocol.

What has to be true

Cooperate, adapt, withstand, and fit the world

Adaptive Persistence asks three questions of a group: can we cooperate, can we adapt, can we tolerate disruption? A fourth condition is that the mix suits how volatile, hazardous and varied the group’s environment is.

What stands in the way

Fixes that treat symptoms

Pressure for visible progress and fear of vulnerability keep fixes on the surface, so conflict returns. Free riders make cooperation hard to sustain.

CauseFixes address superficial concerns while the mismatch between the group's capacities and its environment remains.From Stephen Le (document)CauseMembers feel too unsafe to be vulnerable and reveal their real needs, so assessments rarely reach what people actually think and feel.From Stephen Le (document)CauseFree riders take advantage of other people’s cooperation, making it hard to sustain in the long run.From Stephen Le (document)ProblemFriction and conflict keep returning between members or units, and trust and morale fall.From Stephen Le (document)

What could change

Find the mismatch, then close it

Assess the environment and the group’s capacities, emphasise the capacity the environment calls for, and use the ACT matrix to bring what really gets in the way into the open, with success criteria agreed at the start.

ChangeThe group runs the Adaptive Persistence cycle: orient on one agreed issue, assess its environment and core capacities, analyse the mismatch, adjust, and repeat.From Stephen Le (documents)ChangeEmphasise adaptability where the environment is volatile, robustness where it is hazardous, and coordination where it is neither.From Stephen Le (document)ChangeWork through the ACT group matrix: the values behind the chosen capacity, how the group would act if it improved, the thoughts and feelings that get in the way, and how to move forward while acknowledging them.From Stephen Le (document)ChangeAgree clear success criteria at the start, test each cycle against them, and stop once they are met.From Stephen Le (document)Desired effectThe group's capacities fit its environment better with each cycle.From Stephen Le (document)

What must become possible

An agreed issue and a safe way to adjust

A group needs one issue nobody objects to, shared data on its environment and capacities, someone trained to lead the matrix, and a format and vocabulary that suit it. These are proposed readiness conditions, not completed preparations.

StepThe group agrees one main issue that nobody objects to, can be evaluated, is stated positively, is achievable through action and applies to everyone, with clear success criteria.From Stephen Le (document)StepInterviews, surveys and existing records describe the environment's volatility and hazard and the group's coordination, adaptability and robustness principles.From Stephen Le (document)StepSomeone trained can lead the group through the matrix, from inside the group or as an outside facilitator.From Stephen Le (document)StepAn adjustment format suits the group's size and trust: talking for small high-trust groups, email for small low-trust ones, the matrix with meetings, or voting for large groups.From Stephen Le (document)

How it could be tried

Orient, assess, analyse, adjust, repeat

The summary’s five steps, as an illustrative sequence for one group. The existing conditions are examples, not observations; no group, facilitator, owner or date is assigned.
