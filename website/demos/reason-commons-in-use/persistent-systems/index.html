The theory’s starting point

Why some things last

Persistent Systems Theory asks why some structures keep going while others, under the same battering, stop. Anything that lasts faces three threats; what survives is what a long filter left behind; new levels form when defection stops paying. These cards reconstruct those claims before the AI’s practical map.

ObservationAnything that persists faces three distinct threats: its own parts turning on it, blows from outside, and an environment that changes and does not change back.From Thrival theory compendium (document)ObservationPersistence is a filter, not a goal: the things still here are the ones whose properties happened to keep them here.From Thrival theory compendium (document)ObservationNew levels of organisation are built when defection is made unprofitable, not when cooperation simply breaks out.From Thrival theory compendium (document)

From the documents to a shared question

Persistent Systems Theory says anything that lasts must keep its parts cooperating, absorb shocks and change in time, in proportions its environment rewards. Adaptive Persistence turns that into five steps a group can take. This map follows the practical protocol and keeps the theory’s own warnings beside it.

What has to be true

Cooperate, adapt, withstand, and fit the world

Adaptive Persistence asks three questions of a group: can we cooperate, can we adapt, can we tolerate disruption? The essay adds a fourth condition: the mix has to suit how volatile, hazardous and varied the group’s environment is.

What stands in the way

Fixes that treat symptoms

Pressure for visible progress and fear of vulnerability keep fixes on the surface, so conflict returns. Cutting reserves and experiments looks sensible in good weather and leaves a group exposed to shocks and to quiet obsolescence.

CauseFixes address superficial concerns while the mismatch between the group's capacities and its environment remains.From Stephen Le (document)CauseMembers feel too unsafe to be vulnerable and reveal their real needs, so assessments rarely reach what people actually think and feel.From Stephen Le (document)ProblemFriction and conflict keep returning between members or units, and trust and morale fall.From Stephen Le (document)ProblemA shock the group could have absorbed proves crippling.From Thrival theory compendium (document)ProblemThe group keeps doing well at something its environment no longer rewards, until it is suddenly wrong for where it is.From Thrival theory compendium (document)

The tensions behind the choices

Two dilemmas a group has to work through

Reserves and experiments compete for one budget; openness and self-protection pull against each other in a competitive field. Both are proposed readings of the sources, offered for a real group to test.

What could change

Find the mismatch, then close it

Assess the environment and the group’s capacities, emphasise the capacity the environment calls for, and use the ACT matrix to bring what really gets in the way into the open. Agreed success criteria guard against sorting that teaches nothing.

ChangeThe group runs the Adaptive Persistence cycle: orient on one agreed issue, assess its environment and core capacities, analyse the mismatch, adjust, and repeat.From Stephen Le (documents)ChangeEmphasise adaptability where the environment is volatile, robustness where it is hazardous, and coordination where it is neither.From Stephen Le (document) · Thrival theory compendium (document)ChangeWork through the ACT group matrix: the values behind the chosen capacity, how the group would act if it improved, the thoughts and feelings that get in the way, and how to move forward while acknowledging them.From Stephen Le (document)ChangeAgree clear success criteria at the start, test each cycle against them, and stop once they are met.From Stephen Le (document) · Thrival theory compendium (document)Desired effectThe group's capacities fit its environment better with each cycle.From Stephen Le (document)

What must become possible

An agreed issue and a safe way to adjust

A group needs one issue nobody objects to, shared data on its environment and capacities, someone trained to lead the matrix, and a format and vocabulary that suit it. These are proposed readiness conditions, not completed preparations.

StepThe group agrees one main issue that nobody objects to, can be evaluated, is stated positively, is achievable through action and applies to everyone, with clear success criteria.From Stephen Le (document)StepInterviews, surveys and existing records describe the environment's volatility and hazard and the group's coordination, adaptability and robustness principles.From Stephen Le (document)StepSomeone trained can lead the group through the matrix, from inside the group or as an outside facilitator.From Stephen Le (document)StepAn adjustment format suits the group's size and trust: talking for small high-trust groups, email for small low-trust ones, the matrix with meetings, or voting for large groups.From Stephen Le (document)

A theory and a practice

From why things last to what a group can do

Persistent Systems Theory is a proposal about everything that persists, from cells to companies; its own introduction says the verdict is not yet in. Adaptive Persistence turns its three capacities into a protocol for human groups. This map follows the practical protocol and keeps the theory’s claims, and its warnings about itself, alongside.
