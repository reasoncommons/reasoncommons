A theory and a protocol

Persistent Systems Theory asks why some structures keep going while others, under the same pressures, stop, and proposes three capacities that answer three kinds of threat: coordination against parts turning on the whole, robustness against blows from outside, and adaptation against a world that changes and does not change back (Why Things Last, §§6–9). Adaptive Persistence turns those capacities into a protocol for human groups. The summary places both in a larger Persistence Framework with a meta-ethics, Procedural Ethics, and says the ethics and the theory are not essential to fostering cooperation but help sustain it in the long run (summary, p. 2).This map follows the protocol and keeps three of the theory’s claims as attributed observations, without causal connections. How the theory could be wrong

The essay names five ways it could fail: it might forbid nothing, its three categories might not carve the world at a joint, nothing in it is yet measured, purpose can creep into its language, and it might be a machine for producing plausible metaphors (§15). The map carries this as a risk among the proposed changes, with agreed success criteria as the proposed guard, and as open work: a measure two assessors would agree on and a prediction that could come out false. The case studies

The summary reports four cases from the author’s own facilitation, using lighter ProSocial variations of the method: a genomics nonprofit, a small company and its contractor, a jazz quartet and two leaders of a foodways nonprofit (pp. 8–9). They are the author’s reports, not independent evidence, and the summary itself asks for further cases in competitive, innovative or hazardous settings. Inspect the attributed observation →
