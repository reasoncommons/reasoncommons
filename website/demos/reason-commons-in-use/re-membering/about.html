Selves and capacities taking shape through relating

Heath begins with ongoing bodily, social and material relations. In her process account, selves and their boundaries take shape through those relations; bodies, tools and environments help constitute what someone can perceive and do. The source-account observations preserve that philosophical starting point, distinct from the causal hypotheses in the practical application. See the spoken script, pp. 19–22 and 26, and inspect the attributed claim and its sources.

Following the songline

The talk walks a route through a stick, a doorway, a hammer, a living cell, coordinated movement, a ship’s bridge, a notebook and a river. Each encounter changes the account of where thinking happens. Heath offers this modern songline in tribute to Aboriginal songlines, not as one of them (spoken script, pp. 7–8). Returning to land, memory boards and song, she describes remembering as an activity sustained through bodies, places and practices, with consequences when those relationships are damaged (pp. 24–25).

Two movements of enmindment

Heath distinguishes attuning to a world with tendencies of its own from articulating forms that shape us in return. Her examples are a river whose flow calls for adjustment and a raked garden whose making also changes the maker. Together they describe reciprocal human and nonhuman agency, extending beyond permission or control (spoken script, pp. 4–6 and 26). Inspect the account of agency →

Uncertainty and bringing forth

Some surprise can open new meanings, capacities and possibilities, including changes in what participants seek together. Heath calls this poiesis, or bringing forth (spoken script, pp. 19–23). The practical map therefore asks whether interaction changes purposes as well as the next action. It does not assume that all surprise is beneficial: novelty can also overwhelm or harm. Evidence and philosophical commitments

The script distinguishes evidence for coupling from the contested claim that a tool constitutes part of a mind, and from the author’s philosophical starting point in participation and process. The coupling–constitution objection and predictive-processing boundary debate remain open. The claim that all regulation is co-regulation is the author’s synthesis, not a result established here. Exploring a practical hypothesis does not establish the ontology; the philosophical claims remain visible for examination in their own right.

The spoken script with notes supplies the main qualifications: consent, freedom to step out, harm and repair, and the difference between cited work and Heath’s own extensions. Cultural concepts are situated in living traditions, not treated as interchangeable names for one idea.

From the account to one practical application

The goal, causal explanation, conflict, proposed changes, readiness conditions and action sequence explore a bounded somatic commons. They are the AI’s selected application of the talks, with a reciprocal process loop and attention to bodily, social and environmental timescales. They do not complete the wider account of Body, We and World. The conflict between imposed regulation and collective participation is a candidate dilemma for this application; whether it captures Heath’s emphasis remains an open review question.

What counts as progress?

No willing group, baseline, trial owner or outcome is established. The conflict and readiness dependencies are proposed interpretations. Conditions in the action sequence are illustrative, not observations of an actual host. Before trying a practice, participants would need to agree how to judge support, learning, repair, agency and harmful effects, including whether declining is possible in practice. Assessment also asks what changed in participants’ capacities, purposes and available possibilities. Quietness or compliance alone would not settle those questions. Privacy, consent and override remain safeguards to test, not guarantees.
