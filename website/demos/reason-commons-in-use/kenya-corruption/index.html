The proposed goal

Public money in Kenya is raised, spent and audited openly, and people who abuse public office are held to account.

From the documents to a shared question

Transparency International’s Anti-Corruption Helpdesk surveyed corruption in Kenya in October 2014: pervasive bribery, misspent public money, strong laws weakly enforced and senior officials rarely convicted. This map follows the brief and infers what could change from the gaps it identifies.

What has to be true

Open public money, accountable office

Laws enforced rather than only passed, watchdogs and courts that can act, a budget citizens can follow, clean elections, police who serve, and a press that can expose corruption. The goal is the AI’s inference from the brief’s focus on public financial management.

What stood in the way in 2014

Impunity at the top

Laws were diluted or not implemented, the anti-corruption commission could not prosecute, and courts were overloaded, so senior officials were rarely convicted. Meanwhile most people paid bribes and public trust in the fight fell from 70% to 34%.

ProblemAs of 2014, senior officials implicated in corruption were rarely prosecuted or convicted; the few convictions were for petty cases with lenient sentences.From Samira Lindner, Transparency International (document)CauseAs of 2014, laws meant to put the 2010 constitution into effect had been diluted, and many anti-corruption laws were not implemented.From Samira Lindner, Transparency International (document)CauseThe Ethics and Anti-Corruption Commission could investigate and recover assets but could not prosecute; it forwarded cases for others to prosecute.From Samira Lindner, Transparency International (document)ProblemAs of 2013, petty bribery was pervasive: 70% of Global Corruption Barometer respondents said they had paid a bribe to at least one of eight public services in the past year.From Samira Lindner, Transparency International (document)ProblemConfidence in the government's fight against corruption fell: 70% of respondents rated it effective in 2011, 34% in 2013.From Samira Lindner, Transparency International (document)

The tension behind the choices

Two dilemmas the brief records

Whether to repay donors for stolen school funds from the treasury, and whether to settle out of court to recover assets fast. Each is a proposed reading of the brief.

Settle, or prosecute?

Out-of-court settlements return assets quickly; the brief’s example traded a hotel for dropped proceedings. Prosecution deters, but slowly.

What could change

Bring cases to judgment, and follow the money

Give the commission power to prosecute, resource the courts, restore vetting, put the public finance law into effect and audit every fund. The brief identifies these gaps; the changes are the AI’s inferences from them.

ChangeGive the Ethics and Anti-Corruption Commission power to prosecute the cases it investigates, which the brief notes parliament could do.From Samira Lindner, Transparency International (document)ChangeFund and staff the courts, including the special magistrates for corruption and economic crimes, to clear corruption cases.From Samira Lindner, Transparency International (document)ChangeRestore asset declaration and vetting to the Leadership and Integrity Act, check declarations, and keep people charged with or convicted of corruption out of office.From Samira Lindner, Transparency International (document)ChangePut every regulation of the 2012 Public Financial Management Act into effect, including citizen participation and in-year reports on actual spending.From Samira Lindner, Transparency International (document)ChangeExtend audits to extra-budgetary funds and give the Auditor General the funding and channels to report to the public.From Samira Lindner, Transparency International (document)

What must become possible

Reforms passed intact, bodies that work

Reform has been weakened before it passed and slowed by lack of money. A coalition, public tracking of bills, committed MPs, secure budgets and settled mandates are proposed conditions, not completed preparations.

StepA broad coalition of civil society, religious organisations and think tanks keeps corruption on the public agenda, building on the National Anti-Corruption Campaign.From Samira Lindner, Transparency International (document)StepDrafts and amendments of anti-corruption bills are tracked in public, so weakening changes are seen before they pass.From Samira Lindner, Transparency International (document)StepParliamentarians committed to fighting corruption carry the bills, with support from networks such as the African Parliamentarians Network Against Corruption.From Samira Lindner, Transparency International (document)StepCourts, the Auditor General and police oversight have budgets secured in law.From Samira Lindner, Transparency International (document)
