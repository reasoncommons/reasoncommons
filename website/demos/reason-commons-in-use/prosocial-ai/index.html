The book’s starting point

How worldviews shape AI, and AI shapes us

Atkins and Wilson treat worldviews as cultural replicators that spread through what they reward. AI systems carry those worldviews in their behaviour and teach them back to the people around them, at a moment when human cooperation is still a partial evolutionary transition. These cards reconstruct that framework before the AI’s practical map.

ObservationWorldviews behave as cultural replicators: they last, spread by social learning from successful, prestigious and majority models, and stay faithful because they reward the behaviour that expresses them.From Paul Atkins and David Sloan Wilson (document)ObservationAI systems' observable behaviour embodies the worldviews behind their design, and in turn shapes what engineers build, what executives reward and what users expect.From Paul Atkins and David Sloan Wilson (document)ObservationHuman cooperation is a partial major evolutionary transition: competition within groups has been suppressed only as far as past and present environments selected for, with no finish line.From Paul Atkins and David Sloan Wilson (document)

From the documents to a shared question

Paul Atkins and David Sloan Wilson argue that worldviews, not technology, decide whether AI erodes or builds human capability, and that institutions which make cooperation pay are the way through. This map follows their argument across four scales and keeps their own cautions beside it.

What has to be true

Flourishing at every scale, with cooperation that pays

People, organisations, societies and the planet each need AI that builds rather than extracts. Underneath, institutions have to make cooperation more rewarding than extraction; the book proposes Ostrom’s core design principles for that.

What stands in the way

Extraction that rewards itself

Four extractive worldviews and the pull of immediate rewards shape what institutions reward. AI built to capture attention and do tasks for people erodes their capabilities, firms that externalise harm outgrow the rest, and a social trap locks the pattern in.

CauseFour extractive worldviews dominate AI development: technological determinism, hyper-individualism, market fundamentalism and anthropocentric dominance.From Paul Atkins and David Sloan Wilson (document)CauseInstitutions reward engagement, quarterly profit and fast growth: engineers advance by optimising engagement, executives face earnings pressure and investors back scalable extraction.From Paul Atkins and David Sloan Wilson (document)ProblemPeople's competence, autonomy and relatedness erode: critical thinking declines with heavy AI use, choices are delegated to recommendation systems and chatbot companionship displaces human contact.From Paul Atkins and David Sloan Wilson (document)ProblemFirms that externalise harm outcompete those that do not, and harms documented by internal research are shelved in favour of growth.From Paul Atkins and David Sloan Wilson (document)ProblemA social trap locks extraction in: each adopter gains in the short run while universal adoption leaves everyone worse off and makes reversal harder.From Paul Atkins and David Sloan Wilson (document)

The tensions behind the choices

Two dilemmas, for a firm and for a person

A firm has to keep up with rivals and avoid harming its users; a person wants work done quickly and wants to keep their own skills. Both are proposed readings of the book, offered for real cases to test.

What could change

Make prosocial AI pay, and build capability

Apply the design principles, require capability audits in procurement, measure capability impacts and design AI to scaffold thinking. The authors’ own cautions, such as imposing principles over local wisdom, are kept as risks.

ChangeApply Ostrom's core design principles to AI governance at every scale.From Paul Atkins and David Sloan Wilson (document)ChangeMake public procurement require independent audits showing that an AI system enhances capability.From Paul Atkins and David Sloan Wilson (document)ChangeDevelop measures and monitoring that make AI's effects on capability visible across scales.From Paul Atkins and David Sloan Wilson (document)ChangeDesign AI to scaffold thinking: ask probing questions, add reflection points and teach when to use strategies, rather than hand over finished work.From Paul Atkins and David Sloan Wilson (document)Desired effectSuccess at each scale reinforces success at the others: the life pattern rather than the cancer pattern.From Paul Atkins and David Sloan Wilson (document)

What must become possible

Measures, wider tests and real backing

Drawn from the authors’ own limits: an agreed measure of capability impact, tests beyond Western and democratic settings, independent research funding and demonstrations at scale. These are proposed conditions, not completed work.

An evolutionary argument

The cancer pattern and the life pattern

The book reads AI through multilevel selection: when success at a lower level comes at the expense of the levels above, the result looks like a tumour; when success at each level supports the others, it looks like life. Its claim is that institutions, not virtue, decide which pattern AI follows, and that the next few years matter most.
