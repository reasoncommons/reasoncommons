The Arrows Nobody Checks

Make a claim specific enough to challenge, then let the challenge change the map.

A scrolling piece: the drawings build as you read. It picks up in the clinic from The Wrong Queue, where sixty people are waiting and everybody knows where the problem is.

Illustrated skill conversations accompany the story. Original essay-only edition.

The method, with the skill in use

Reason Commons helps an AI assistant keep a reasoning map: claims, authors, objections and the next check. A “skill” is an installed package of instructions and tools for the assistant.

Ashfield and these conversations are invented. Inspect a real execution record from this revision, including its saved state and limits.

Sam manages; Maria runs reception and follow-up; Tomasz triages; Dr Adeyemi consults; Leila runs the pharmacy. This fictional team discusses operational flow.

Dr Adeyemi has an explanation for the lost time, and she can show her working.

Every prescription needs her countersignature. So the pharmacist interrupts her eleven times a morning. So consultations overrun.

She thinks those overruns push clinical notes into the evening. Missing information then makes later consultations harder. Each connection needs checking.

It is an opinion with a structure: a chain whose connections could be wrong somewhere specific.

Four other people are carrying chains just as detailed, and they arrive somewhere else entirely.

The practice manager's ends at funding. A nurse's ends at the ten-minute appointment rule. Maria's ends at the referral backlog.

They know different parts of the same clinic.

And not one of those chains has ever been outside the head that made it.

Five people have useful experience. The clinic needs to turn their explanations into claims it can compare and investigate.

Otherwise the discussion can go to whoever is most senior, most confident, or least tired at four o’clock.

Try it yourself, on something small.

Take something you believe about why something in your life isn't working. Why the team misses deadlines. Why the meetings are useless. Why you never get to the gym.

Write it down as a sentence: X causes Y.

The belief that felt crisp in your head may go vague on paper. Writing a particular connection makes it possible to ask where it might fail.

Nothing has changed except that it is now outside you, where it can be looked at.

Intuition is fast, rich and often right. An unstated explanation is harder for someone else to inspect.

Sam’s instinct says the real problem here is funding. What does he mean, and what would distinguish that explanation from the others?

Evidence can change an intuition. Writing the explanation down makes the relevant evidence easier to identify, and the revision easier to retain.

Now — plenty of people already draw diagrams. Flowcharts. Mind maps. Boxes on a whiteboard with arrows between them.

And people do argue with them. That shouldn't come before this. That box is wrong.

Without a standard for checking an arrow, the objection can become another opinion. A diagram alone does not settle it.

Make the arrows checkable: ask what the claim means, what it depends on, and what would count against it.

What would make us revise this arrow?

So here are the rules. They are less a technique than a set of questions you are allowed to ask about any claim anybody makes.

Do I understand you? Not do I agree — do I know what you mean. A great many arguments that look like disagreement are two people using one word differently and getting steadily angrier about it.

Is that a claim at all? A complete thought, one idea, that could be true or false. "Funding" is not a claim.

Is that cause enough on its own? If we have fuel and a heat source, then we have fire. True? No. You forgot oxygen — because it is always there.

Could something else have done it? The same effect can have several causes. Removing one does not necessarily remove the others.

Have you got it backwards? Anglers on the bank did not cause good fishing. They are how you know it. The cause is that the river was stocked on Tuesday.

If you’re right, what else would be true? If a later budget cut created this queue, comparable records from before the cut should not show the same longstanding queue.

And are you going in a circle?

A recollection becomes an assigned check, rather than a second confident opinion.

Every argument has an oxygen: the condition so ordinary that nobody names it, right up until the day it isn't there.

Confusing the sign for the cause is how organisations end up managing their indicators instead of their problems. It is how you end up hiring a receptionist because the queue is at the desk.

Seven questions. Notice what almost none of them are about.

You do not need to run a clinic to ask is that cause enough on its own? The question concerns the argument in front of both people. Answering it may still need their expertise.

A clear question tells the team which expertise or records it needs.

And here something changes that has nothing to do with logic.

Criticism can feel personal when nobody can point to the particular inference they dispute. The meeting generates heat instead of light.

When the reasoning is out on the page, the page takes the criticism.

Two people can stand side by side, looking at the same diagram, hunting for the flaw together. Dettmer's phrase for it: not you against me, but you and I against the problem.

I don't see how this cause is sufficient — what am I missing? is not an insult. It's a legal move.

It does not always work. People may refuse to update, and a diagram can be gamed. Surviving objections makes an explanation worth using provisionally; it does not prove it true.

Back at Ashfield, Sam and Tomasz check the waiting records together.

The 2018 and recent records use the same waiting measure and show similarly long waits. The staffing and demand records do not reveal an obvious change that explains away the earlier queue. This is a comparison, not a controlled experiment.

Sam withdraws the claim that the 2019 cut created the longstanding queue. Its later effect remains an open question in the working record.

Notes on sources

The eight rules are the Categories of Legitimate Reservation, set out in H. William Dettmer, The Logical Thinking Process (ASQ Quality Press, 2007), chapter 2: clarity, entity existence, causality existence, cause insufficiency, additional cause, cause-effect reversal, predicted effect existence and tautology. Seven are used here; two are merged, and the names are dropped in favour of the questions they ask.

The fuel/heat/oxygen and anglers examples are adapted from Dettmer; he credits the anglers example to Charles M. Johnson. The blank-screen example and fictional funding prediction are this revision’s illustrations. Dettmer also describes unchecked diagrams as “somebody’s perception of the relationship” and frames scrutiny as “not you against me, but you and I against the system.”

Ashfield, its budget change, records and conversations are invented. The earlier waiting comparison challenges a particular timing claim, not every possible funding effect. The notes/interruption observations motivate a trial; they do not establish a unique root cause. The worked prediction example now uses the clinic rather than a categorical medical diagnosis.

The conversations are invented; no patient information is used. Desktop scenes illustrate the installed skill; phone scenes illustrate an explicitly invoked team-chat prototype. Proposed means not endorsed. Accepted for now names a person willing to work from the claim; it is neither proof nor group consensus. Reopening the same actual file preserves continuity; independent copies are not automatically shared.
