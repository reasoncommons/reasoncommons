Five Shapes

Five questions any real change has to answer — and why they need differently shaped answers.

The method, with the skill in use

Reason Commons helps an AI assistant keep a reasoning map: claims, authors, objections and the next check. A “skill” is an installed package of instructions and tools for the assistant.

Ashfield and these conversations are invented. Inspect a real execution record from this revision, including its saved state and limits.

Sam manages; Maria runs reception and follow-up; Tomasz triages; Dr Adeyemi consults; Leila runs the pharmacy. This fictional team discusses operational flow.

The clinic has a shared map with one revised claim and several live explanations. The later budget cut did not create the earlier queue. Its present effect remains open.

The team has found overruns on days without pharmacy interruptions, and searching when records are missing. Ten minutes is the booked slot; eighteen is the observed average consultation. Preparation might explain part of that gap. They will test it before changing the schedule or committing the hiring budget.

Good. Now what?

Because why are we stuck is only one of the questions you need answered, and it isn't even the first one.

What should be happening? Not what's wrong. What does success actually look like — and what has to be true for it to be possible at all?

Tomasz proposes: people who need care can get it, with good consultations and fewer avoidable repeat visits.

Counting appointments alone will not tell them whether they succeeded. They must also check quality, avoidable repeat work and the burden on staff.

The skill selects a goal question because the team needs a criterion for judging changes.

Until that sentence exists, "the problem" is whatever irritates whoever is talking. Everyone measures the place against a private, unstated idea of what it should be, and then argues about the gap.

You cannot have a useful argument about a gap when nobody has agreed where the far side is.

Why isn't it happening? Waits too long. Staff exhausted. Avoidable repeat work. Referrals late.

The instinct is to fix these one by one, and that instinct is the trap.

They may share causes. Or some may need different changes. The map makes those possibilities explicit instead of assuming one answer.

Work backwards from each symptom: what could produce it, and what would support that connection? Look for shared causes while keeping alternatives available.

Here the missing-record explanation could account for some overruns and duplicate work. The hospital referral queue may have a different cause. Neither connection becomes true merely because it fits on the map.

So why hasn't somebody already fixed it?

The ten-minute schedule protects the number of available appointments. Dr Adeyemi wants time for good consultations. Changing it could help one aim while harming another.

Usually a conflict. Not a personality clash — a structural one, where the thing hurting you is protecting something else you also need.

The clinic needs access to care and consultations that hold up. Necessary follow-up remains part of good care.

Sam favors keeping short slots for access. Dr Adeyemi favors longer slots for quality. Both needs deserve to survive the discussion.

You do not pick a side. You ask what would have to be true for these two to be genuinely opposed — and you write those assumptions down.

Must better consultations always mean longer slots? Preparing existing information might remove avoidable searching and duplicate history-taking. It will not remove the examination or the doctor’s clinical notes.

Challenge the assumption, and another option may become possible.

  • Assumption challengedMore scheduled minutes are the only way to improve consultations.
  • Alternative · proposedPrepare existing information; preserve clinical judgment and test the consequences.

The challenged assumption stays beside the alternative the team will test.

Goldratt called this way of examining a conflict an evaporating cloud. Finding an alternative is a start; the team must still test whether it meets both needs.

Will it actually work? Try preparing existing records before clinic, while leaving the schedule unchanged for a bounded trial.

Ready information could reduce searching and avoidable repetition. That might reduce some overruns; it does not guarantee a ten-minute consultation.

Less overrun might leave more time for the doctor’s new clinical notes. Each arrow is a prediction, with other causes still possible.

Then the question people skip because they don't want the answer: what else does it do?

Maria estimates forty minutes each morning to locate and prepare the existing records. She cannot absorb that on top of reception. The trial needs covered time, and someone must count its cost.

Finding the cost before committing the hire lets them change the plan while it is still small.

A better plan preserves the useful prediction without quietly transferring the problem to Maria.

Expected benefit, possible harm and responsibility are inspectable before the outcome is known.

What do we do on Monday, and in what order?

An idea becomes a solution when it is implemented and doing what it was meant to do. The road between those two states is where most good ideas are buried.

So: what stands in the way? Not a list of tasks — a list of obstacles, honestly stated.

The preparation must be ready before eight; it needs a clear standard.

The person who could do it is already the busiest in the building.

There is still money for one hire. The team has not committed it.

First cover a small reassignment for a two-week trial. If the benefit justifies the cost, the hire could provide preparation support.

Tomasz and Dr Adeyemi draft the preparation standard by Friday. Sam arranges the forty-minute reception cover and logs the management work it displaces. Then Maria can try it. The order matters: the standard and cover must exist before the trial starts.

Five questions, and each needs a differently shaped answer: a hierarchy for the goal, a web of cause and effect for the diagnosis, a small diagram of opposing needs for the conflict, the same causal logic run forwards for the test, and a chain of obstacles for the plan.

Together they are called the Logical Thinking Process. Goldratt built them; Dettmer spent decades teaching and refining them.

The questions are easy to state. Checking their answers takes records, time and judgment. The skill helps the team retain and organize that work.

The diagrams are not the product. Nobody needs more diagrams.

The useful record includes the claim, its author, the objection, what was checked and the question still open. Later, someone can reopen that same working file and ask why the plan changed. The answer should not depend on everyone remembering the meeting.

Claims with addresses — so a disagreement can be found, examined and revisited when the evidence changes.

Two weeks later, in this fictional follow-up, the team reviews what happened.

They compare equal-length periods using the same preparation and waiting measures, checking the rota and demand for obvious differences. Records are ready earlier and staff report less duplicate history-taking. Some consultations still overrun. The hospital referral wait has not visibly changed.

The small, nonrandom comparison cannot isolate the cause or justify a permanent hire yet. It does answer one practical question: preparation can be covered without asking Maria to do two jobs at once. The cost stays beside the reported benefit.

Notes on sources

The five tools are H. William Dettmer's, in The Logical Thinking Process: A Systems Approach to Complex Problem Solving (ASQ Quality Press, 2007): the Intermediate Objectives Map (chapter 3), the Current Reality Tree (chapter 4), the Evaporating Cloud (chapter 5), the Future Reality Tree (chapter 6) and the Prerequisite Tree (chapter 7). Their names are omitted here in favour of the questions they answer; anyone going on to use the method will meet the terms soon enough.

The retired sixth tool is the Transition Tree. Dettmer writes in chapter 7 that over ten years of teaching he found it "the least valuable of the Thinking Process tools for nearly all practitioners," and replaces it with a more detailed Prerequisite Tree, critical-chain project management, and a chapter on the human element in change. The observation that an unstated goal reduces the question of what to change to "a matter of opinion and speculation" is from chapter 3; that conflict is "generally rooted in the hidden, underlying assumptions operating on each side" is from chapter 5; "ideas are not solutions" is from chapter 1.

The entire Ashfield case is fictional, including its observations, two-week trial, mixed follow-up and conversations. It illustrates how to record a prediction and review a limited comparison, not evidence of clinical improvement or a recommended appointment policy. Prepared existing information is distinct from new clinical documentation; necessary repeat visits remain part of care. No clinical outcome or permanent-hire decision is established. The separate execution record documents actual software/helper behavior during this revision.

The conversations are invented; no patient information is used. Desktop scenes illustrate the installed skill; phone scenes illustrate an explicitly invoked team-chat prototype. Proposed means not endorsed. Accepted for now names a person willing to work from the claim; it is neither proof nor group consensus. Reopening the same actual file preserves continuity; independent copies are not automatically shared.
